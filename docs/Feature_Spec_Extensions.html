Feature Spec — Extensions

Two ideas that would change the modelling task, kept out of the main spec on purpose. Nothing here is planned work.

Why these are in a separate document. The approved proposal commits to movement features into an XGBoost skill classifier with SHAP. Both ideas below would change that, so mixing them into the feature spec would quietly widen the scope. The main spec assumes none of this, and stays valid whether or not either is ever adopted. Adopting one should be an explicit decision with the mentor, not a drift.

1. Within-match comparison

The idea. Instead of asking "is this player high or low skill," take two players from the same match and ask which one is ranked higher.

What it would fix

Section 6 of the main spec measures a real problem in the current plan: 76% of the variation in a player's rank is explained by which match they were in, because matchmaking groups similar players. Any feature computed from the other nine players is partly a read on the lobby, so a model can score well without learning anything about the individual.

Within a single match the lobby is identical for both players being compared, so that route is closed by construction. Whatever separates them has to come from their own behaviour. It also removes the match-level confounders we cannot otherwise control: game length, tempo, patch, who the opponents were.

What it would cost

AspectDetail
Extra data collectionNone. Runs on the same 500 matches.
Sample sizeUp to 45 pairs per match, so roughly 22,500 before filtering.
LabelRelative, so class balance is exact by construction — randomise which player is presented first.
Pair filteringOnly pairs at least one full medal apart. Medals lag actual skill and Dota tracks separate MMR per role, so smaller gaps are mostly noise. The average match spans two medals, so plenty qualify.
New workA pairwise feature representation (differences between the two players' feature vectors) and a different evaluation story — pair accuracy rather than class accuracy.
Where it sits today. This is on the standup agenda as a question to the team, framed as a possible second analysis alongside the main model — not as a replacement for it. If the team says no, the main spec is unaffected; section 6's teammate-only control still tells us how much of the main model is lobby-reading.

2. Policy-based (reinforcement / imitation learning) framing

The idea. Treat each player as following a policy: at every 2-second step they observe a situation and take a movement. Fit a policy to high-ranked players' behaviour, then score an unseen player by how likely their movements are under it. Skill becomes "how closely does this player behave like a strong player in the same situation."

The L1 table in the main spec happens to be the right shape for this — per-step situation plus the movement taken — but that is a side effect of building it for aggregation, not a reason to pursue this.

Why it is not straightforward here

It inherits the leakage problem and adds one of its own. The situation a player observes is built almost entirely from where the other nine players are — exactly the information that identifies the lobby. On top of that, a low likelihood score has two causes the method cannot separate: the player chose a different movement in a situation strong players also face, or the player was in a situation strong players rarely reach at all. Someone repeatedly pushed back into their own base scores badly because their situations are unusual, not because their movements are. Separating the two requires comparing movements within matched situations, which is a research problem in itself.

Other gaps between what this needs and what we have:

  • We do not know what the player could see. Vision is central to Dota decisions, and we extract neither wards nor fog of war. Any fitted policy is conditioned on information the player may not have had.
  • We observe outcomes, not decisions. We see where a hero ended up, not the order that was issued, and nothing about items or abilities. The "action" is an approximation of the decision.
  • 500 matches is a small corpus for a policy model, which is hungrier than a seventy-feature tabular model.
  • Evaluation does not transfer. Accuracy, AUC and SHAP — the metrics the proposal commits to — do not apply. We would need likelihood-based comparisons and a new baseline argument.

What would have to be true to adopt it

  • The main deliverable is finished and evaluated, with time genuinely left over.
  • The mentor agrees the scope change is worth it, since it replaces the committed evaluation approach rather than adding to it.
  • We accept a fallback: if it does not work, it is written up as an attempt with negative results, not as a gap in the MVP.
The low-cost version. Describe the framing in Future Work and point out that the data layout already supports it. That costs a paragraph, makes the report end on a forward-looking note, and commits us to nothing.

3. Recommendation

Neither of these should displace the current plan. The immediate priority is the one that applies regardless: handling the leakage route in section 6 of the main spec — running the teammate-only control, deciding whether relational features stay, and grouping the split by match_id.

Of the two, the within-match comparison is the cheaper and better-motivated one, because it addresses a measured problem rather than adding a new capability. It is already on the standup agenda as a question. The policy framing is best left in Future Work unless the main deliverable lands early.
